tesla

I am currently working as a mechanical design engineer on the drive systems team, focusing on high voltage distribution. I am the design owner of multiple parts on the vehicle side charging system for Cybercab and future vehicles.

I work on developing new vehicle programs, from high level planning and integration with large groups, to detailed, innovative part design, to ramping and manufacturing at a massive scale.

Actuator integration

I own the design and integration of an actuator system, which I have developed from an existing design to a better, more reliable product. I have dealt with every issue you can think of: electrical contact degradation, poor sealing, corrosion, environmental wear, motor burnout, fatigue failures, signal corruption, supplier inconsistencies, not enough grease, too much grease, the wrong kind of grease, and on and on.

I have designed and soldered custom electronics to test actuators, have spent hours analyzing oscilloscope traces, and many more hours decoding CAN signals to characterize field failures.

The work is ongoing as I investigate new designs and actuator types to increase reliability, decrease cost, and improve performance.

Ramping to massive scale

My parts do not terminate as ideas and prototypes, they are actively scaling.

I have learned that the ramping process is far more intense than the design process, it turns out 0->1 is a breeze compared to 1->1,000,000. The manufacturing process magnifies weak points and teases out the 5%, 1%, and 0.1% failures. Identifying them and fixing them early and rapidly is so important, and it's something I have learned to do at Tesla. An example of this:

During ramp, the factory flagged a design problem with a part that caused a vehicle EOL issue. I dropped everything and spent the next few days designing a tester to screen out affected parts. A teammate flew a prototype to the factory and got feedback, and I hand soldered a production-worthy version which I flew to our upstream assembler and then trained line workers to use it. This contained the issue and allowed me to validate a robust design change which would get rid of the problem completely.

Early design and prototyping

Speed is the name of the game. In my experience, the sooner you can have a working model running representative firmware, the closer you are to rev 2. I 3D print it, destroy it, modify it, and print it again. I have also learned to be bold with designs and trying out new technology when the timeline permits it.

Innovative and careful design for manufacturing

Anything that can go wrong in production will go wrong. Taking the effort to carefully make an engineering drawing that lists every part requirement and lays out a precise testing strategy at the tier 1 and tier 2+ suppliers is something that pays itself back 50x later in the manufacturing process.

Full vehicle development

I was deeply involved in the early development stages of a future vehicle, working with a group of other subsystem owners to establish the vehicle architecture from the ground up to meet the design objectives. I learned to voice our requirements and flag issues early, and that people are much more likely to compromise on their designs to give you what you need through an in-person talk than over messages. It is a tight balance of using existing proven technologies and taking a risk on something new for meaningful performance gains.

fsae formula e

I was a founding member of the FSAE Formula Electric team at BYU. Within two years, we built a functional car and sent it to competition and it passed accumulator safety check (a very difficult task for a rookie team).

Year 1

In the first year, me and a group of equally ambitious and unqualified students designed the electric powertrain and built it for a trial chassis. We used a custom designed and built-to-spec pack and an off the shelf inverter and motor. Year 1 was super scrappy, it was nowhere close to rules compliant, and we were barely able to get the motor spinning, but it did spin! It was the necessary foundation for the next year and exposed the areas that I needed more knowledge in.

Year 2

This is when it got real. Me and a group of 8 other engineers designed and built a custom 450v accumulator (battery pack) from scratch. Me and a friend Landon were the only ones who had been part of the high voltage team in year one.

The areas I owned were overall pack layout, collector plates with integrated fuses, thermal sensing, and BMS integration. In each of these areas I led design discussions, performed experiments and simulations, made decisions, and documented my work.

Here is some of my work for the collector plates I did. One key FSAE rule was that every cell must have its own fuse to prevent parallel groups from igniting if one cell has an internal short. I integrated these fuses into the collector plates to save significant cost and assembly time. To design and validate these collectors, I wrote matlab functions which simulated the thermal model from first principals. I used these results as a starting point, and used a custom (and sketchy) testing rig to precisely verify the fusing time of my prototypes at different currents. Please note the handmade water cooled stainless steel coil resistor with adjustable resistance. Much consideration was also put towards manufacturing, spot welding compatibility, and cost.

haptic audio controller

I am working on a bluetooth device that sits on my desk and gives physical interfaces to basic audio controls for music and meetings. I am incorporating a little turntable-style platter that turns as audio plays through it and can be used to scratch back over a song or meeting you are in (inspired by Teenage Engineering OB-4). I'm always listening to music and connecting to meetings through my phone, but I don't want to have it out all the time to handle controls.

My technical motivations for the project:

  • Buttery smooth haptic interfacing
  • Use field-oriented control on a brushless DC motor with a magnetic encoder
  • Low latency communication and processing (<10ms)
  • PCBA design
  • Industrial design for the housing

Architecture

  • Built around an ESP32-S3 chip, quick processing speeds and double cores were a key requirement (core 0 strictly handles motor control and feedback, core 1 handles audio buffer, screen, user input)
  • Motor is a brushless DC gimbal motor driven by a DRV8313 three-phase driver, with field-oriented control running on the ESP32 via the open-source SimpleFOC library and a 12-bit AS5600 magnetic encoder for rotor angle. I spent a long time on motor choice, and considered using a small brushed motor with physical gearing and clutch, similar to a real turntable, but I decided on a gimbal motor because of the total integration and small packaging of the motor, magnetic encoder, and mechanical structure. I also found the SimpleFOC open-source library which unlocked precise control at very low speeds and has an active community which I referenced for the firmware. I've spent a long time tuning the control method and filtering for this to make the feel correct with very low cogging. Tying an audio output to motor velocity makes this matter a lot; high frequency wobble you can't physically feel shows up as noticeable audio distortion in the highs and lows.
  • I want this to be powered by a usb-c connection that can be plugged into a laptop, so the power draw is limited. This governed the max reaction torque of the platter motor, and required me to add a boost converter to bump up the voltage for my motor. During normal operation the closed-loop motor controls run at shockingly low power (<0.1 watts) so system cooling is not a concern.
  • Firmware is based around three main processing systems:
    • Audio and visuals: In core 0 on the ESP32, audio is pulled in through a bluetooth connection from a coprocessor chip and fed into a ring buffer which overwrites itself every two minutes. The audio is fed out through a DAC to a lined connection, the audio position and rate of output is set by a pointer which references the motor position and velocity.
    • Motor controls: In core 1, the motor is controlled using a closed loop system using simpleFOC library's from which reference the resolver.
    • Bluetooth: The ESP32 only has BLE 5, which is the latest and greatest bluetooth standard, but it is so late and great that it won't connect to your phone :(. This meant I needed a coprocessing chip with an older BT standard that then communicates with the main system over I2C.

    I defined the architecture, timing budget and control approach, used Claude Code to write most of the firmware, then debugged and tuned it on hardware. This system took a lot of refining, I added a velocity filter and a calibration cycle that the motor spins through during boot to measure cogging due to the permanent magnets, which it then compensates for. Thanks to the simpleFOC community for that one. I really wanted to do bluetooth in and bluetooth out to airpods, but airpods make this so unfriendly, and it added too much complexity and mostly would have made the latency too high to be usable in a meeting or get a responsive feel at the turntable.

This project is in process.

beauty as data compression

I recently read and was deeply influenced by this paper written by a researcher named Jurgen Schmidhuber in 2009. The title is "Driven by Compression Progress: A Simple Principle Explains Essential Aspects of Subjective Beauty, Novelty, Surprise, Interestingness, Attention, Curiosity, Creativity, Art, Science, Music, Jokes". The paper is worth the read, but in summary, Schmidhuber lays out a general mathematical definition of subjective beauty and interestingness, defining the beauty of a set of data as the quality of being compressible in a way where it can be described in much fewer bits. This suggests why we find beauty in patterns and symmetrical faces. Interestingness, by this framework, is the derivative of beauty. If new data is helping us to understand, describe, and compress our current inputs as well as our recorded memories, and therefore find them more beautiful, it is interesting until it is no longer useful in improving our compression method.

To further understand and apply the idea, I built a single-level, linear autoencoder that processes vision data and gives an output of the perceived beauty and interestingness of each picture, and uses the image to train its internal data compression scheme. It is self motivated to maximize interestingness and data compression progress. For my input vision data, I used images from the Phenocam database, which pulls from thousands of stationary cameras pointing at nature all around the world capturing 16-48 images a day. It’s mostly pictures of crops; this data is primarily used to analyze vegetation cycles. A possible future of this project is to build several solar powered long-lifetime vision/compute units that I can place in a nice spot and they will stay there for 30+ years. They will watch and analyze the world for beauty, selectively looking at what they find interesting, training their unique data compression models. They will be disconnected from their radio frequency antennas so there can not be any output. I think beauty only exists when perceived, so maybe increasing the compute power dedicated to perceiving beauty will increase the amount of beauty in the world.
